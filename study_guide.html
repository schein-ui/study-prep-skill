Lewis Structures & Covalent Bonds

7th Grade Science · Page 1 of 2

The Big Idea

Atoms share electrons to form covalent bonds. When atoms share, they make a molecule. We can draw molecules two different ways:

  • Dot diagram — every shared and unshared electron is shown as a dot ( : or · ).
  • Line diagram (also called a structural formula) — each shared pair of electrons is replaced by a single line ( — ).

Both pictures show the same molecule. One line = one shared pair = one covalent bond.

Key Vocabulary

Valence electrons: the electrons in an atom's outer shell. These are the only electrons that form bonds.
Covalent bond: a chemical bond where two atoms share a pair of electrons.
Lone pair: two valence electrons on an atom that are not shared (drawn as two dots together).
Molecule: two or more atoms held together by covalent bonds.

The Two Big Rules

1. Octet Rule

Most atoms are most stable when their outer shell holds 8 electrons. They share to reach 8.

2. Duet Rule (just for Hydrogen)

Hydrogen only needs 2 electrons in its outer shell — never 8. It can form just one bond.

Tip: Count valence electrons by group number on the periodic table. Group 1 → 1, Group 14 → 4, Group 17 → 7.

Example 1 — Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl₄)

Dot diagram

·· :Cl: ·· ·· ·· ·· ·· :Cl: ·· C ·· :Cl: ·· ·· ·· ·· :Cl: ··

Each pair of dots between C and a Cl is one shared bond. Each Cl has 6 more dots (3 lone pairs).

Line diagram

Why this works (electron count)

  • Carbon brings 4 valence electrons. Each chlorine brings 7. Total = 4 + (4 × 7) = 32 electrons.
  • Carbon shares 1 electron with each Cl, so C ends up sharing 8 electrons total → octet ✓.
  • Each Cl shares 1 electron with C and keeps 6 unshared (3 lone pairs). 2 shared + 6 lone = octet ✓.
  • 4 single bonds × 2 e⁻ = 8 bonding electrons. 4 Cl × 6 lone e⁻ = 24 lone electrons. 8 + 24 = 32 ✓.

Example 2 — Silane (SiH₄)

Dot diagram

H ·· ·· H ·· Si ·· H ·· ·· H

Each pair of dots between Si and an H is one shared pair = one bond. No lone pairs anywhere.

Line diagram

Why this works (electron count)

  • Silicon brings 4 valence electrons. Each hydrogen brings 1. Total = 4 + (4 × 1) = 8 electrons.
  • All 8 electrons are used in 4 single bonds. No lone pairs anywhere — that's why the line diagram looks so clean.
  • Si is sharing 8 electrons → octet ✓. Each H is sharing 2 electrons → duet ✓.

⚠ Watch Out — Common Mistakes

Don't put lone pairs on hydrogen. H only has 1 valence electron and follows the duet rule. After it makes one bond, it's done.
Don't forget the lone pairs on chlorine. Each Cl has 3 lone pairs (6 dots) plus the shared pair. Leaving them off is the most common CCl₄ mistake.
One line = one shared pair (2 electrons), not 1 electron. A double line (=) would mean 4 shared electrons, but CCl₄ and SiH₄ only have single bonds.
Carbon has 4 valence electrons, not 6. Group number on the periodic table tells you valence electrons for main-group atoms (C is in Group 14 → 4).

Quick Practice (cover the answers!)

  1. How many valence electrons does silicon have? → 4
  2. How many bonds does hydrogen normally make? → 1 (duet rule)
  3. In CCl₄, how many lone pairs are on each Cl atom? → 3
  4. In a line diagram, what does one straight line represent? → one shared pair of electrons = one covalent bond
  5. Why do C and Si form similar molecules (CCl₄ and SiCl₄, CH₄ and SiH₄)? → They're in the same group (14) and both have 4 valence electrons.
  6. How many total valence electrons are in a SiH₄ molecule? → 8 (4 from Si + 4 × 1 from H)
  7. True or False: Hydrogen needs 8 electrons to be stable. → False — only 2.

Memory Tricks

"H is a duet, the rest want octet."
H stops at 2; everyone else aims for 8.
"Group ones-digit = valence #" for main-group atoms.
Group 1 → 1, Group 14 → 4, Group 17 → 7.
"Lines hide pairs."
Every line in a structural formula is just two dots in disguise.
