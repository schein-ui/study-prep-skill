Central dogma	DNA → (transcription) → RNA → (translation) → Protein
Transcription — where + enzyme	In the nucleus, by RNA polymerase
Translation — where + organelle	In the cytoplasm, at the ribosome
Promoter	DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds to START transcription
mRNA	Messenger RNA — carries the message from nucleus to ribosome
tRNA	Transfer RNA — brings the right amino acid to the ribosome (has anticodon)
rRNA	Ribosomal RNA — makes up the ribosome itself
Codon	3-base mRNA sequence that codes for one amino acid
Anticodon	3-base tRNA sequence that pairs with a codon
Start codon	AUG (also codes for methionine / Met)
Stop codons	UAA, UAG, UGA
3 differences DNA vs RNA	DNA double / deoxyribose / T  vs  RNA single / ribose / U
Silent mutation	Substitution where amino acid stays the same — no effect
Missense mutation	Substitution that changes one amino acid
Nonsense mutation	Substitution that creates a premature STOP codon
Frameshift mutation	Insertion or deletion shifts the reading frame — usually devastating
Differentiation	Process by which cells become specialized in structure and function
Diploid (2n)	Cell with 2 sets of chromosomes — humans have 46 (23 pairs)
Haploid (n)	Cell with 1 set of chromosomes — human gametes have 23
Tetrad	4-chromatid structure formed in Prophase I from a homologous pair
Crossing over	Exchange of segments between homologous chromatids during Prophase I
Independent assortment	Random alignment of homologous pairs at Metaphase I → genetic variation
Spermatogenesis	Makes 4 sperm per meiosis
Oogenesis	Makes 1 egg + 3 polar bodies per meiosis
Why halve chromosomes in meiosis?	So fertilization restores the correct diploid number (n + n = 2n)
Why only 1 egg per meiosis?	All cytoplasm goes to the egg to support a future embryo
Mitosis vs Meiosis (3 differences)	Mitosis = 1 round, 2 identical 2n cells, no crossing over | Meiosis = 2 rounds, 4 unique n cells, crossing over
Gene	Segment of DNA that codes for a trait
Allele	A version of a gene (e.g., purple allele or white allele)
Homozygous	Two identical alleles (PP or pp)
Heterozygous	Two different alleles (Pp) — also called hybrid
Genotype	The genetic makeup — the letters (Pp)
Phenotype	The physical trait you see (purple flowers)
Mendel's Law of Segregation	The two alleles for a gene separate into different gametes
Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment	Alleles for different genes sort into gametes independently
Monohybrid heterozygous cross	Pp × Pp → 3:1 phenotype ratio, 1:2:1 genotype
Dihybrid heterozygous cross	TtYy × TtYy → 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio
Incomplete dominance	Heterozygote shows BLENDED phenotype (red + white = pink)
Codominance	Heterozygote shows BOTH phenotypes (AB blood type)
Multiple alleles	A gene with more than 2 alleles in the population (blood: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i)
Polygenic trait	Trait controlled by multiple genes — gives a bell-curve distribution
X-linked inheritance	Genes on X chromosome — recessive X-traits much more common in males
Why colorblindness more in males?	Males XY have only 1 X — no second X to mask a recessive allele
Karyotype	Picture of all chromosomes lined up — shows sex (XX vs XY) and abnormalities
Autosomal	Any of the 22 non-sex chromosome pairs
Evolution	Change in the heritable characteristics of a population over generations
Natural selection	Individuals with traits suited to environment survive and reproduce more
Fitness	Reproductive success — how many offspring you leave (NOT physical strength)
Adaptation	Heritable trait that increases fitness in the environment
Artificial selection	Humans breeding organisms for desired traits (dog breeds, crops)
Homologous structures	Same anatomy, different function — evidence of common ancestry (human arm + bat wing)
Analogous structures	Different anatomy, same function — evolved independently (bee wing + bat wing)
Vestigial structures	Inherited body parts with little or no function (appendix, whale pelvic bones)
Hutton & Lyell	Geologists — argued Earth is very OLD; gave Darwin enough time for evolution
Lamarck	Proposed inheritance of acquired traits (mechanism wrong, but species do change)
Malthus	Populations grow faster than resources → struggle for existence
3 patterns of biodiversity (Darwin)	Species vary globally · vary locally · vary over time (fossils)
Directional selection	One extreme favored — bell curve SHIFTS
Stabilizing selection	Middle favored — bell curve NARROWS
Disruptive selection	Both extremes favored — bell curve SPLITS
Genetic drift	Random changes in allele frequency, esp. in small populations
Bottleneck effect	Population drastically reduced — survivors carry only some alleles
Founder effect	Small group starts new population — their alleles set the new baseline
Speciation	Formation of a new species
Reproductive isolation	Populations can no longer interbreed (required for speciation)
Behavioral isolation	Different courtship rituals (meadowlark songs)
Geographic isolation	Physical barrier (canyon separates squirrels)
Temporal isolation	Reproduce at different times
Cladogram	Branching diagram showing evolutionary relationships; nodes = common ancestors
Biosphere	All life on Earth + everywhere life exists
Biome	Group of ecosystems with similar climate (desert, tundra)
Ecosystem	Community + abiotic environment
Community	All populations of different species in one area
Population	Members of one species in one area
Species	Group that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring
Niche	Full role of a species (where lives, what eats, how interacts) — its "job"
Habitat	The physical place where a species lives — its "address"
Abiotic	Non-living factors (water, sun, soil, temperature, air)
Biotic	Living factors (plants, animals, fungi, bacteria)
Autotroph	Makes own food via photosynthesis or chemosynthesis (producers)
Heterotroph	Must eat other organisms (consumers)
Herbivore	Eats only plants
Carnivore	Eats only animals
Omnivore	Eats both plants and animals
Scavenger	Eats animals already dead (vulture)
Decomposer	Chemically breaks down dead matter (bacteria, fungi)
Detritivore	Eats small bits of decaying matter (worms)
Food chain	Linear path of who eats whom
Food web	Network of interconnected food chains
Trophic level	One step in a food chain — producers are always level 1
10% Rule	Only ~10% of energy passes from one trophic level to the next (rest = heat)
Mutualism	+/+ relationship — both species benefit (bee + flower)
Commensalism	+/0 relationship — one benefits, other unaffected (barnacle on whale)
Parasitism	+/− relationship — one benefits, other harmed (tapeworm in dog)
Competitive exclusion principle	No two species can occupy exactly the same niche in the same place
Keystone species	Disproportionately large effect on community despite low abundance (sea otters)
Invasive species	Non-native species causing harm (pythons in Everglades)
Carrying capacity	Maximum population an environment can sustainably support
Density-dependent factor	Limiting factor that strengthens with crowding (competition, disease, predation)
Density-independent factor	Limiting factor that hits regardless of size (weather, natural disaster)
Primary succession	Starts on bare rock — no soil (after volcano). Pioneer: lichens. Slow.
Secondary succession	Starts where soil remains after a disturbance (after fire). Pioneer: grasses. Faster.
Pioneer species	First species to colonize a barren area
Climax community	Stable end state of succession
Biogeochemical cycle	Cycle that moves matter through the biosphere (water, C, N, P)
Why energy flows but matter cycles	Energy lost as heat at each step (1-way); matter recycled by decomposers
Testes	Male gonads — produce sperm + testosterone (in scrotum, cooler than body)
Vas deferens	Tube carrying sperm from testes to urethra
Prostate	Gland producing seminal fluid (nourishes sperm)
Urethra (male)	Carries BOTH urine and semen out through penis
Ovaries	Female gonads — produce eggs + estrogen + progesterone
Fallopian tubes	Passageway from ovary to uterus — SITE OF FERTILIZATION
Uterus	Where the embryo implants and develops
Cervix	Lower opening of uterus into vagina
Placenta	Pregnancy organ — transfers O₂ + nutrients mom→baby, removes CO₂ + wastes
Umbilical cord	Connects fetus to placenta
FSH	From pituitary. Females: matures follicle. Males: stimulates sperm production.
LH	From pituitary. Females: triggers ovulation (~day 14). Males: triggers testosterone.
Estrogen	From ovaries — drives oogenesis, uterine lining, female 2° sex characteristics
Progesterone	From corpus luteum — thickens uterine lining for implantation
Testosterone	From testes — drives sperm + male 2° sex characteristics
Menstrual cycle phases	Follicular → Ovulation (~day 14) → Luteal → Menstruation (if no fertilization)
Right atrium	Receives DEOXYGENATED blood from body (via vena cavae)
Right ventricle	Pumps DEOXYGENATED blood to lungs (via pulmonary artery)
Left atrium	Receives OXYGENATED blood from lungs (via pulmonary veins)
Left ventricle	Pumps OXYGENATED blood to entire body (via aorta) — strongest chamber
Aorta	Largest artery — carries oxygenated blood from left ventricle to body
Vena cavae	Largest veins — return deoxygenated blood from body to right atrium
Pulmonary artery	Carries DEOXYGENATED blood from right ventricle to lungs (only artery to do so!)
Pulmonary vein	Carries OXYGENATED blood from lungs to left atrium (only vein to do so!)
Pulmonary circuit	Heart ↔ lungs (drops CO₂, picks up O₂)
Systemic circuit	Heart ↔ body (drops O₂, picks up CO₂)
Artery	Carries blood AWAY from heart — thick walls, high pressure
Vein	Carries blood TO heart — thinner walls, has VALVES to prevent backflow
Capillary	One cell thick — site of gas/nutrient exchange with tissues
Plasma	Liquid part of blood — carries dissolved substances
Red blood cells	Carry O₂ via hemoglobin
White blood cells	Fight infection
Platelets	Help blood clot
Path of O₂ molecule	Nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli → capillary → hemoglobin
Alveoli	Tiny sacs at end of bronchioles — SITE of gas exchange
Why alveoli are great at gas exchange	Huge surface area, walls 1 cell thick, moist, dense capillary network
Cilia + mucus role	Mucus traps junk, cilia sweep it UP and OUT toward throat
Diaphragm role in inhaling	Contracts and flattens DOWN → expands chest → drops pressure → air IN
Why breathe through nose better	Nose warms, moistens, filters air (mucus + cilia)
Medulla oblongata	Brainstem region that controls breathing — monitors CO₂ levels
Epiglottis	Flap covering trachea when you swallow (keeps food out of airway)
Trachea	Windpipe — tube from larynx to bronchi, lined with cilia
Bronchi	Two big tubes branching from trachea, one to each lung
Bronchioles	Smaller branches off the bronchi
